# Every named character Frodo meets in *The Fellowship of the Ring*

**From Bag End to Parth Galen, Frodo Baggins encounters more than thirty distinct named characters**—hobbits, wizards, Elves, Dwarves, Men, and stranger beings besides. What follows is a chronological catalog of every one of them, drawn strictly from Tolkien's published prose. Each entry reconstructs the character as the text presents them: how they look, how they speak, how it feels to be in their presence, and what role they play in the wider story of Middle-earth.

---

## 1. Bilbo Baggins — the uncle who started everything

Bilbo is **111 years old** at the Long-Expected Party, yet looks scarcely different from a hobbit of fifty. Tolkien writes that at ninety people called him "well-preserved," but "unchanged would have been nearer the mark." He stands between three and four feet tall, as all hobbits do, with the curly brown hair, bright eyes, red cheeks, and clever brown fingers characteristic of his kind. His feet are bare and leathery-soled, furred on top. Despite his unnaturally youthful exterior, Bilbo confesses to Gandalf that he feels "all thin, sort of stretched… like butter that has been scraped over too much bread"—a weariness the Ring has inflicted even as it preserved his body.

Bilbo is rich, generous, and considered delightfully peculiar by Shire standards. He speaks with educated, witty hobbit diction, fond of riddling wordplay. His farewell speech is a masterpiece of genial self-amusement: "I don't know half of you half as well as I should like; and I like less than half of you half as well as you deserve." He calls the Ring "my precious" in a moment that alarms Gandalf with its echo of Gollum. A writer, poet, and scholar of Elvish, Bilbo carries the Tookish streak of adventure beneath a veneer of Baggins respectability.

When Frodo finds him again **seventeen years later in Rivendell**, Bilbo has visibly aged. Without the Ring's preservation, time has caught up. He dozes by the fire, composes poetry, and works on his translations. When he asks to see the Ring, Frodo perceives him for an instant—through the Ring's distorting influence—as "a little wrinkled creature with a hungry face and bony grasping hands," a vision that foreshadows Gollum and reveals how deeply the Ring's shadow still clings to its former bearer.

---

## 2. Gandalf the Grey — swift in anger, quick to laugh

Tolkien introduces Gandalf driving a cart into Hobbiton: "An old man… He wore a tall pointed blue hat, a long grey cloak, and a silver scarf. He had a long white beard and bushy eyebrows that stuck out beyond the brim of his hat." This portrait is almost verbatim from *The Hobbit*, deliberately anchoring the new story to the old. Later, at Rivendell, Tolkien enriches the picture: **"Gandalf was shorter in stature than the other two; but his long white hair, his sweeping silver beard, and his broad shoulders, made him look like some wise king of ancient legend. In his aged face under great snowy brows his eyes were set like coals that could suddenly burst into fire."**

Gandalf stands roughly five foot six—tall enough to tower over hobbits, short enough to duck through their doors with effort. He carries a gnarled staff that serves as both walking stick and instrument of power, and he smokes a long pipe with evident relish. His grey robes are plain but imposing; a poem in the book captures his essence perfectly: "A lord of wisdom throned he sat, swift in anger, quick to laugh; an old man in a battered hat who leaned upon a thorny staff."

His personality oscillates between warmth and thunder. He delights in entertaining hobbit-children with fireworks, speaks with deep fondness of simple things, yet can become terrifyingly stern—his voice darkening, his frame seeming to swell with shadow when he rebukes Bilbo over the Ring. He is "quick to anger, and equally quick to laugh," possessed of "great pity and patience." His preferred method is encouragement and persuasion rather than raw force. He walks at "a surprising pace" despite his stooped appearance, and Frodo notices him looking "unusually bent, almost as if he was carrying a great weight" when he departs. Gandalf is, in truth, an incarnate angelic being—Olórin, a Maia of Valinor—sent to oppose Sauron, though he wears mortality like a heavy cloak.

---

## 3. Lobelia Sackville-Baggins — the umbrella and the fury

Tolkien gives Lobelia no detailed physical portrait, but her presence is unmistakable. She is a hobbit-woman of ordinary stature—roughly three feet tall—defined entirely by her ferocious personality and her **umbrella**, which functions as her scepter, her weapon, and occasionally a tool for concealing pilfered silverware. When she arrives to claim Bag End after Bilbo's departure, Frodo catches her "investigating nooks and corners and tapping the floors," searching for hidden treasure. She has "snatched some small valuable things in her umbrella" before being found out. Her parting insult to Frodo cuts with the worst venom a Shire hobbit can muster: "You're no Baggins—you—you're a Brandybuck!"

Lobelia is sharp-tongued, covetous, snobbish, and domineering—the quintessential difficult relative. She has coveted Bag End for decades and bitterly resents both Bilbo and Frodo for possessing it. Yet Tolkien gives her a surprising arc of redemption beyond the scope of this volume: she later attacks Saruman's ruffians with that same umbrella, endures imprisonment, and ultimately gives Bag End back to Frodo, leaving her money to help displaced hobbits. She dies having finally earned the Shire's admiration—proof that even the most disagreeable characters in Tolkien possess hidden reserves of courage.

---

## 4. Lotho Sackville-Baggins — sandy-haired and pimply

Lotho appears only briefly at Frodo's side. Tolkien describes him as Lobelia's **"sandy-haired son"** and gives him the withering nickname "Pimple" among his fellow hobbits, indicating a notably poor complexion. He is greedy, ambitious, and power-hungry—a chip off the Sackville-Baggins block taken further than even his mother might approve. His story darkens far beyond the boundaries of this book: he eventually sets himself up as "the Chief" of the Shire in a petty totalitarian regime, only to become Saruman's puppet and ultimately his victim.

---

## 5. The Gaffer (Hamfast Gamgee) — gossip and good sense

Frodo encounters Sam's father holding forth at the Ivy Bush inn, where the Gaffer speaks "with some authority, for he had tended the garden at Bag End for forty years." He is old, stiff in the joints, garrulous, and deeply loyal to the Baggins family. "A very nice well-spoken gentlehobbit is Mr. Bilbo, as I've always said," he declares, and he is conservative in the way of practical country folk: "Elves and Dragons! Cabbages and potatoes are better for me and you." He warns Sam against getting "mixed up in the business of your betters," though his own courage later shows when he gives the Nazgûl false information and refuses their demands without flinching.

---

## 6. Samwise Gamgee — plain speech, uncommon heart

Sam is plumper than Frodo, with the standard hobbit features: curly brown hair, bright eyes, work-worn brown hands from years of gardening. Tolkien describes him as "a simple Hobbit of plain speech," but this simplicity conceals a love of Elves, a gift for poetry, and a stubborn devotion that will prove the salvation of Middle-earth. He uses rustic, working-class Shire dialect—"sir" and "Mr. Frodo" at all times, folksy expressions inherited from the Gaffer. "Don't you leave him! they said to me. Leave him! I said. I never mean to." He is shy, socially awkward, ferocious in a fight, and always thinking about rope, food, and the practicalities of survival. Tolkien considered Sam "the chief hero" of the story. His relationship with Frodo mirrors that of a **World War I batman** to his officer—loyal, self-effacing, and utterly indispensable.

---

## 7. Peregrin "Pippin" Took — golden curls and youthful folly

Pippin is the **youngest and smallest** of the four hobbits, born in T.A. 2990, making him just twenty-eight at the quest's beginning—not yet of age by hobbit reckoning, still in his "tweens." Tolkien distinguishes him physically by noting that "the hair on his head and feet (which were exceptionally hairy even for a Hobbit) was curly and almost golden," fairer than the brown hair typical of his kind. He is cheerful, curious, and impulsive—a troublemaker with a good heart who has not yet learned to temper his curiosity with wisdom. He is the natural accomplice to Merry's schemes, though he "showed his age" in moments of danger. His speech is light-hearted and sometimes flippant, using humor to mask fear, and his arc across the trilogy traces the most dramatic maturation of any character in the Fellowship.

---

## 8. Meriadoc "Merry" Brandybuck — the perceptive one

Merry is brown-haired (not blond, as the films suggest), the son of the Master of Buckland, and the **most intelligent and organized** of the younger hobbits. His Westron name, Kalimac, means "handsome, happy." At thirty-six, he is a full adult by hobbit standards—three years older than the age of majority. He is the mastermind behind "the Conspiracy," the group of friends who secretly watched over Frodo and prepared for his departure. He loves boats, ponies, and maps; he has a practical, strategic mind and speaks with the quiet confidence of his social standing. As a Brandybuck, he is considered slightly "peculiar" by other Shire hobbits—the Bucklanders live near the Old Forest, lock their doors at night, and are comfortable with water, all traits that mark them as exotic by Shire standards.

---

## 9. Fredegar "Fatty" Bolger — the courage of the one who stays behind

Fatty's nickname is his most concrete physical description: he is plumper than average even for a hobbit. Born in the same year as Sam, he is one of the Conspirators who know about the Ring and support Frodo's departure. Tolkien defines him as a lover of food and comfort who "had never left the Shire" and was terrified of the Old Forest. He refuses to enter it—"Fatty was adamant"—despite Merry's argument that "the Old Forest would be nothing compared to meeting the Black Riders." Yet when the Nazgûl come to Crickhollow, where he has stayed as a decoy wearing Frodo's clothes, he is "frightened nearly witless" but manages to escape out the back door and raise the Horn-call of Buckland. "They little thought how dangerous that part might prove," Tolkien writes of the friends who left him behind. His courage is the quiet kind—staying to face a terror everyone else fled from.

---

## 10. Gildor Inglorion — starlight on the road

Frodo meets Gildor and his company of Elves on the road in the Woody End, and the encounter is defined less by individual description than by collective radiance. Tolkien writes that the hobbits could see **"the starlight glimmering on their hair and in their eyes. They bore no lights, yet as they walked a shimmer, like the light of the moon above the rim of the hills before it rises, seemed to fall about their feet."** Gildor identifies himself as "Gildor Inglorion of the House of Finrod"—a Noldorin Elf, one of the Exiles, whose people are "now only tarrying here a while, ere we return over the Great Sea."

Gildor's personality is wise, cautious, and diplomatically evasive. He hails Frodo as "Elf-friend" upon recognizing his knowledge of Elvish, but refuses to give direct counsel about the Black Riders: "Elves seldom give unguarded advice, for advice is a dangerous gift, even from the wise to the wise." He coins one of Tolkien's most quoted maxims: **"Do not meddle in the affairs of Wizards, for they are subtle and quick to anger."** When Frodo wryly retorts, "Go not to the Elves for counsel, for they will say both no and yes," Gildor laughs with genuine delight. He is gracious, hosting the hobbits with bread, fruit, and a fragrant drink in a woodland clearing that seems a "great hall of trees." His intervention is pivotal: his company's presence drives off a lurking Nazgûl, and he sends word to both Tom Bombadil and Rivendell about Frodo's danger.

---

## 11. Farmer Maggot — earth under his old feet

When Tolkien introduces Farmer Maggot, the description is characteristically physical and precise: **"Through the gate there now appeared a broad thick-set hobbit with a round red face."** He is a Stoor-type hobbit of the Marish, living in a house rather than a burrow (the land is too flat and wet for tunneling), and likely wearing boots—hobbits of the Eastfarthing wore "dwarf-boots in muddy weather." His three huge dogs—**Grip, Fang, and Wolf**—are fierce in reputation but obedient to his word, though even they cower and bolt when a Nazgûl approaches.

Maggot is one of Tolkien's most warmly drawn minor characters. Tom Bombadil says of him: **"There's earth under his old feet, and clay on his fingers; wisdom in his bones, and both his eyes are open."** He is brave enough to confront a Black Rider directly—"This lane don't lead anywhere, and wherever you may be going, your quickest way will be back to the road"—and shrewd enough to refuse the gold offered for information about "Baggins." He speaks in a rustic dialect, plain and direct, and beneath a gruff exterior lies deep generosity: he invites the hobbits to supper, drives them to the Bucklebury Ferry, and sends Frodo off with a parting gift—a basket of mushrooms from Mrs. Maggot.

---

## 12. Tom Bombadil — oldest and fatherless

Tom Bombadil receives one of Tolkien's richest physical descriptions. He first appears "hopping and dancing along the path," and above the reeds comes into view **"an old battered hat with a tall crown and a long blue feather stuck in the band."** Then the full figure emerges: **"He was too large and heavy for a hobbit, if not quite tall enough for one of the Big People… stumping along with great yellow boots on his thick legs, and charging through grass and rushes like a cow going down to drink. He had a blue coat and a long brown beard; his eyes were blue and bright, and his face was red as a ripe apple, but creased into a hundred wrinkles of laughter."** In his hands he carries white water-lilies on a large leaf.

Tom stands roughly four feet tall, immensely broad—perhaps three feet wide—with thick curling brown hair, **big brown-skinned hands**, and an energy that fills every room he enters. His clothing is vivid and invariant: bright blue jacket, great yellow boots, a green girdle, and leather breeches. In his house, he replaces the battered hat with a crown of autumn leaves. His own song codifies his look: "Old Tom Bombadil is a merry fellow; bright blue his jacket is, and his boots are yellow."

Tom's speech is almost entirely **song, verse, and rhyme**—"Hey dol! merry dol! ring a dong dillo!"—and his voice carries actual power. Through singing he commands Old Man Willow to release the hobbits and banishes the Barrow-wight from its tomb. His personality is the word "merry" made flesh: joyful, whimsical, profoundly ancient yet childlike. He calls himself "Eldest"—"Tom remembers the first raindrop and the first acorn… he knew the dark under the stars when it was fearless—before the Dark Lord came from Outside." The Ring has **no power over him whatsoever**: he puts it on and does not vanish, he sees Frodo when Frodo is invisible, and when he holds it up, the hobbits catch a comical and alarming glimpse of "his bright blue eye gleaming through a circle of gold." Goldberry explains his nature with two words of infinite depth: **"He is."**

---

## 13. Goldberry — the River-daughter

Goldberry sits in Tom's house amid floating white water-lilies, and Tolkien paints her in flowing natural imagery rather than conventional features: **"Her long yellow hair rippled down her shoulders; her gown was green, green as young reeds, shot with silver like beads of dew; and her belt was of gold, shaped like a chain of flag-lilies set with the pale-blue eyes of forget-me-nots."** She is "slender as a willow-wand," and Tom sings of her as "clearer than the water." Tolkien notably omits any description of her skin, eye color, or facial features—she exists as a pure impression of color, movement, and water-music.

Her voice, heard from a distance, is **"young and as ancient as Spring, like the song of a glad water flowing down into the night from a bright morning in the hills."** When she moves, "her gown rustled softly like the wind in the flowering borders of a river." At goodnight, the sound of her footsteps is "like a stream falling gently away downhill over cool stones in the quiet of night." Frodo is so moved by her presence that he spontaneously composes verse: "O slender as a willow-wand! O clearer than clear water! O reed by the living pool! Fair River-daughter!" He recognizes her beauty as different from Elvish enchantment—"less keen and lofty… but deeper and nearer to mortal heart; marvellous and yet not strange." She is called the "River-woman's daughter," and embodies the seasonal rhythms of nature itself.

---

## 14. The Barrow-wight — cold hand and cold incantation

The Barrow-wight is not named but is vividly described. On the hilltop, Frodo first sees **"a tall dark figure like a shadow against the stars"** with "two eyes, very cold, though lit with a pale light that seemed to come from some remote distance." Then "a grip stronger and colder than iron seized him. The icy touch froze his bones." Inside the barrow, a pale greenish light reveals the entombed hobbits dressed in white burial garments with gold circlets and chains. The wight manifests primarily as a disembodied **long arm "walking on its fingertips,"** creeping toward the swords, and its severed hand is later seen "still wriggling" independently.

The wight's incantation is one of Tolkien's most chilling passages of verse: **"Cold be hand and heart and bone, and cold be sleep under stone: never more to wake on stony bed, never, till the Sun fails and the Moon is dead."** Its voice is described as cold, rising and falling, "seeming to be far away high in the air or under the ground, railing against the mornings and warmth it could not have but for which it hungered." These evil spirits were sent by the Witch-king of Angmar to inhabit the ancient burial mounds of Cardolan, animating the bones of dead Dúnedain and older peoples. Only Tom Bombadil's commanding song can banish them.

---

## 15. Harry Goatleaf — the gatekeeper who trembled

The keeper of Bree's western gate questions the hobbits "gruffly" upon their arrival. Tolkien provides no physical description, but Harry's behavior is telling: **"Hobbits! Four hobbits! And what's more, out of the Shire by their talk,"** he mutters, "softly as if speaking to himself," staring at them "darkly." He is nervous, jumpy, and suspicious—compromised not by greed but by terror. Strider later reveals that the Nazgûl spoke to Harry days earlier, leaving him "white and shaking." He warns the hobbits cryptically: "You'll find maybe that more folk than old Harry at the gate will be asking you questions." He is a reluctant informer, eventually siding with the Ruffians and being expelled from Bree.

---

## 16. Barliman Butterbur — the innkeeper who forgot

Tolkien draws Butterbur with affectionate precision: **"a short fat man with a bald head and a red face,"** wearing a white apron, "bustling out of one door and in through another, carrying a tray laden with full mugs." He is perpetual motion personified, and his speech tumbles out in breathless, apologetic cascades: "One thing drives out another, so to speak. I'm that busy tonight, my head is going round." He forgot Gandalf's urgent letter for **three months**. Gandalf's assessment is both fond and exasperated: "A worthy man, but his memory is like a lumber-room: thing wanted always buried."

Butterbur is good-hearted, honest, and genuinely hospitable. His inn has special hobbit-sized rooms. He pays for Bill Ferny's overpriced pony from his own pocket when the hobbits' mounts are driven off. He is not brave by nature—the Nazgûl terrify him—but he is defiant within his own sphere: "Spooks or no spooks, they won't get in The Prancing Pony so easy." Strider describes him with acid affection as "one fat man who lives within a day's march of foes that would freeze his heart, or lay his little town in ruin, if he were not guarded ceaselessly"—unaware of how much he owes to the Rangers' protection.

---

## 17. Nob — the hobbit who improvised

Nob is a hobbit servant at the Prancing Pony, characterized entirely through action rather than appearance. Butterbur summons him as "you woolly-footed slow-coach," suggesting the typical hairy hobbit feet. Nob proves resourceful and brave: he creates decoy beds using bolsters and brown woollen mats shaped into fake hobbit heads, and he ventures out into the dangerous night to search for the missing Merry, finding him unconscious near Bill Ferny's house. He is good-natured—grinning when he nearly slips and says Frodo's real name—and speaks in the working-class dialect of Bree.

---

## 18. Bill Ferny — swarthy, sneering, and treacherous

Strider identifies Bill Ferny in the common room as **"a swarthy sneering fellow"** who "has an evil name in the Bree-land" and "would sell anything to anybody; or make mischief for amusement." When the hobbits depart Bree, Tolkien gives a full portrait: Ferny stares over a hedge with **"heavy black brows, and dark scornful eyes; his large mouth curled in a sneer."** Nearby, through a window, lurks his associate—a different man with "a sallow face with sly, slanting eyes," one of the Southern spies.

Ferny is cruel as well as treacherous: his pony Bill is "a poor old half-starved creature," evidence of habitual neglect. He sells information to the Nazgûl, profiteers from the hobbits' desperation (demanding twelve silver pennies—triple the animal's worth—for the starving pony), and sneers at them as they leave. Sam gets the last word, planting an apple squarely on Ferny's nose—"a waste of a good apple," Sam says, though it is the most satisfying throw in the book.

---

## 19. Aragorn, called Strider — the king in exile

Aragorn's first appearance is one of Tolkien's greatest character introductions. Frodo spots him in the corner of the Prancing Pony's common room: **"a strange-looking weather-beaten man, sitting in the shadows near the wall… He had a tall tankard in front of him, and was smoking a long-stemmed pipe curiously carved. His legs were stretched out before him, showing high boots of supple leather that fitted him well, but had seen much wear and were now caked with mud. A travel-stained cloak of heavy dark-green cloth was drawn close about him, and in spite of the heat of the room he wore a hood that overshadowed his face; but the gleam of his eyes could be seen as he watched the hobbits."**

When he throws back his hood: **"a shaggy head of dark hair flecked with grey, and in a pale stern face a pair of keen grey eyes."** He is tall and lean—nicknamed Strider for his "long shanks"—and everything about him speaks of years spent in the wild. His clothing is worn but well-fitted; a sword hangs concealed beneath his cloak. His manner oscillates between watchful silence and sudden authority. When he reveals his identity, he "seemed suddenly to grow taller. In his eyes gleamed a light, keen and commanding." Then his face softens with "a sudden smile": "But I am the real Strider, fortunately… I am Aragorn son of Arathorn; and if by life or death I can save you, I will."

Aragorn possesses dry, self-deprecating wit—"I look foul and feel fair. Is that it?"—and speaks with deliberate precision, always saying he is "called" Strider rather than claiming the name as his own. He is simultaneously the most dangerous and the most protective figure Frodo has yet encountered. Frodo's instinct proves sound: "I think one of his spies would—well, seem fairer and feel fouler, if you understand." The Appendices note that in his years of exile he "became somewhat grim to look upon, unless he chanced to smile; and yet he seemed to Men worthy of honour, as a king in exile."

## 20. Glorfindel — the Elf-lord who blazed with white light

Glorfindel rides out of the twilight on a white horse named Asfaloth, and Tolkien describes the encounter with rare luminosity: the horse gleams in the shadows, its headstall "flickering and flashing as if studded with gems like living stars." The rider's cloak streams behind him, hood thrown back, **golden hair flowing in the wind, and "white light seemed to shine through the form and raiment of the rider, as if through a thin veil."** At Rivendell, Tolkien elaborates: Glorfindel is **tall and straight**, with a face "fair and young and fearless and full of joy," bright keen eyes, and a voice like music. "Wisdom sat upon his brow, and strength was in his hand."

Glorfindel is a lord of the Noldor who died fighting a Balrog during the Fall of Gondolin in the First Age, was re-embodied by the Valar, and sent back to Middle-earth with spiritual power approaching that of the Maiar themselves. Despite this staggering pedigree, he is humble and decisive, treating a pursuit of three Nazgûl across the Bridge of Mitheithel as routine: "Three of the servants of Sauron were upon the Bridge, but they withdrew and I pursued them westward." His fearlessness is so fundamental that Tolkien embeds it in his physical description itself—it is part of his face, not merely his behavior.

---

## 21. Elrond Half-elven — ageless as twilight

Tolkien's description of Elrond is among his most celebrated passages: **"The face of Elrond was ageless, neither old nor young, though in it was written the memory of many things both glad and sorrowful. His hair was dark as the shadows of twilight, and upon it was set a circlet of silver; his eyes were grey as a clear evening, and in them was a light like the light of stars."** He appeared "venerable as a king crowned with many winters, and hale as a tried warrior in the fullness of his strength. He was the Lord of Rivendell and mighty among both Elves and Men."

Elrond is the living voice of history—an eyewitness to events stretching back thousands of years, half-elven son of Eärendil the Mariner, twin brother of Elros (first King of Númenor), former herald of Gil-galad who stood on the slopes of Mount Doom when Sauron was overthrown. He speaks with archaic, measured gravity: "Such is oft the course of deeds that move the wheels of the world: small hands do them because they must, while the eyes of the great are elsewhere." He is a healer of extraordinary skill—he pulls Frodo back from the edge of becoming a wraith—and bears **Vilya**, the greatest of the Three Elven Rings. His manner is wise, kind, and profoundly weary with the weight of ages, accepting that destroying the One Ring may diminish the power of his own realm forever.

---

## 22. Arwen Undómiel — the Evenstar glimpsed

At the feast in Rivendell, Frodo sees Arwen for the first time. She sits at a place of honor, "so like was she in form of womanhood to Elrond" that Frodo guesses their kinship immediately. Tolkien's description is deliberately catalogued, feature by feature: **"Young she was and yet not so. The braids of her dark hair were touched by no frost; her white arms and clear face were flawless and smooth, and the light of stars was in her bright eyes, grey as a cloudless night; yet queenly she looked, and thought and knowledge were in her glance, as of one who has known many things that the years bring."** Above her brow sits a cap of silver lace netted with small gems, "glittering white," and her soft grey raiment bears no ornament except a girdle of leaves wrought in silver.

Tolkien writes that "such loveliness in living thing Frodo had never seen before nor imagined in his mind." She is called Undómiel—Evenstar of her people—and bears the likeness of Lúthien Tinúviel returned, which is both her glory and her doom. She speaks little in the main text of *The Fellowship of the Ring*; her presence is more symbolic than vocal, a silent resonance of the ancient love-story that Aragorn carries in his heart.

---

## 23. Glóin — the old companion, richly dressed

At the feast in Rivendell, Frodo finds himself seated next to **"a dwarf of important appearance, richly dressed. His beard, very long and forked, was white, nearly as white as the snow-white cloth of his garments. He wore a silver belt, and round his neck hung a chain of silver and diamonds."** Frodo stops eating to stare. This is Glóin—one of Thorin Oakenshield's original thirteen companions from *The Hobbit*—now 235 years old and still vigorous. He rises, bows, and introduces himself with the formal Dwarven courtesy: "Glóin at your service." He is talkative about Erebor's prosperity, warm toward Frodo because of Bilbo, and grave when speaking of Balin's lost colony in Moria, from which no word has come in over twenty years. He has come to Rivendell with his son Gimli on an errand from King Dáin, seeking counsel about a sinister messenger of Sauron who appeared at the gates of the Lonely Mountain.

---

## 24. Lindir — the Elf who could not tell mortals apart

Lindir appears only briefly in the Hall of Fire after Bilbo's recitation of his Eärendil poem. He gently teases Bilbo, claiming he cannot distinguish which parts were composed by Bilbo and which by Aragorn—"to sheep other sheep no doubt appear different… or to shepherds. But Mortals have not been our study." Despite the gentle ribbing, he clearly enjoys the performance and requests it again. His name derives from the Sindarin word for "tune," suggesting he is a minstrel. Tolkien provides no physical description whatsoever.

---

## 25. Boromir — the proud son of Gondor

Boromir's arrival at Rivendell is one of Tolkien's most visually striking introductions. He sits a little apart from the other Council members: **"a tall man with a fair and noble face, dark-haired and grey-eyed, proud and stern of glance."** He wears rich garments stained with long travel, a cloak lined with fur, high boots, and **a collar of silver in which a single white stone is set**. His hair is "shorn about his shoulders." On a baldric he carries **a great horn tipped with silver**, which he lays across his knees. He has traveled 110 days on foot to reach Rivendell, having lost his horse at Tharbad—a journey that speaks to his extraordinary physical stamina.

Boromir is a born warrior who delights chiefly in arms, taking no wife, caring "little for lore save the tales of old battles." He speaks with passionate directness: "Gondor wanes, you say. But Gondor stands, and even the end of its strength is still very strong." His love for his city and people drives everything—including, ultimately, his desire for the Ring, which stems from patriotism rather than personal ambition. He clashes with Gandalf and questions Aragorn's claim, but there is no pettiness in him, only the fierce pride of a man defending the only bulwark between Sauron and the West.

At **Parth Galen**, Tolkien charts his corruption with terrible precision. His behavior grows uneasy throughout the journey from Lothlórien; Pippin notices "a strange glint in his eye." When he confronts Frodo alone, his manner shifts from soft persuasion to naked aggression: **"his fair and pleasant face was hideously changed; a raging fire was in his eyes."** After Frodo escapes, Boromir returns to himself—"A madness took me, but it has passed"—and weeps. His final act, defending Merry and Pippin to the death, pierced by many arrows with his sword in hand and twenty slain enemies around him, is among the most moving passages Tolkien ever wrote.

---

## 26. Legolas Greenleaf — the least-described member of the Fellowship

Tolkien introduces Legolas at the Council of Elrond as simply **"a strange Elf clad in green and brown"**—and this is, remarkably, almost the full extent of his physical description in the published text. His hair color is never explicitly stated, creating one of the great debates in Tolkien scholarship: a reference to his "dark head" occurs in darkness and may reflect a hood, while his father Thranduil is described in *The Hobbit* as having golden hair. As a Sindarin Elf, dark hair would be typical of his lineage, but the matter remains genuinely ambiguous. He is tall, lithe, and immensely strong; he wears light shoes that allow him to walk atop snow, and carries a bow of Mirkwood and a long white knife.

In personality, Legolas is **keen-sighted and keen-eared** beyond even other Elves, light-hearted and cheerful, the most tireless of the Fellowship. He speaks sparingly but perceptively, shows wonder at natural beauty, and feels the "moans of stones" in Moria. He came to Rivendell as a messenger to report Gollum's escape from the Wood-elves' custody, and was chosen to represent the Elves in the Fellowship. His philosophical nature emerges in quiet observations: "For the Elves the world moves, and it moves both very swift and very slow."

---

## 27. Gimli son of Glóin — fire in his eyes

Gimli appears at the Council as "a younger dwarf at Glóin's side"—Tolkien provides no detailed portrait beyond establishing him as stout, strong, and distinctly younger than his white-bearded father. He wears a mail-shirt (alone of the Company) and carries a broad-bladed axe. When the possibility of finding his kinsman Balin arises in Moria, **"a smouldering fire was in his eyes."**

Gimli's personality is fierce, proud, and deeply emotional—quick-tempered but capable of profound tenderness. His most famous line at the Council captures his character perfectly: **"Faithless is he that says farewell when the road darkens."** He speaks with Dwarven bluntness, chants the Song of Durin for Sam, takes offense when Dwarven craft is slighted, and declares he "needed no map" to name the peaks of the Misty Mountains. His initial hostility toward Legolas over the ancient Elf-Dwarf enmity gradually evolves into one of literature's great friendships—a transformation catalyzed by his overwhelming response to Galadriel's beauty, one of the most unexpectedly moving moments in the trilogy.

---

## 28. Erestor — the devil's advocate

Erestor is "the chief" among Elrond's counsellors, introduced sitting beside Glorfindel at the Council. Tolkien gives him **no physical description**. His personality emerges through his role at the Council, where he functions as a pragmatic voice: he suggests Tom Bombadil might guard the Ring, then sharply summarizes the Council's only two options—hide the Ring or unmake it—noting both seem beyond their power. He is analytical, somewhat pessimistic, and serves as a useful foil to the more visionary speakers.

---

## 29. Galdor of the Havens — Círdan's messenger

Galdor is introduced as "an Elf from the Grey Havens who had come on an errand from Círdan the Shipwright." Again, Tolkien provides **no physical description**. Galdor asks probing, practical questions at the Council: he demands proof that Frodo's ring is truly the One Ring, inquires about Saruman's absence, and warns that the Ringwraiths will return. He is the voice of cautious skepticism, representing the perspective of the westernmost Elves who stand closest to the Sea and farthest from the Shadow.

---

## 30. Haldir — the marchwarden at the border

The first Elf the Fellowship meets in Lothlórien is Haldir, who challenges them from a tree-platform (a *flet* or *talan*) on the northern borders. He and his brothers **Rúmil and Orophin** wear grey hooded cloaks—the same style later gifted to the Fellowship. Tolkien provides no specific description of his face, hair, or features. Haldir is distinguished primarily by his **linguistic skill** (unlike his brothers, he speaks fluent Westron, having traveled beyond Lórien) and his **wariness toward Dwarves**: he insists that Gimli be blindfolded, a diplomatic crisis resolved only when Aragorn suggests all the Company submit to the same indignity. He is authoritative, courteous but firm, and embodies the watchful isolation of the Galadhrim. He later guides the Fellowship to Caras Galadhon and, after Galadriel overrides the blindfold requirement, graciously apologizes to Gimli.

---

## 31. Celeborn — the Silver Tree

Celeborn sits beside Galadriel in their tree-chamber, and Tolkien describes the pair together: they are **both very tall, clad wholly in white, grave and beautiful**. Celeborn's distinguishing feature is his **hair of silver, long and bright**, and his eyes—shared with Galadriel—are described as "keen as lances in the starlight, and yet profound, the wells of deep memory." There is "no sign of age upon them, unless it were in the depths of their eyes."

Celeborn is a Sindarin Elf-prince, kinsman of Thingol, and grandfather of Arwen through their daughter Celebrían. He is noble and lordly but **more rigid in his judgments** than Galadriel—a quality Tolkien noted as "his failing, perhaps." When he learns the Balrog was disturbed in Moria, he initially blames the Dwarves and regrets admitting Gimli: Galadriel gently corrects him. He is gracious after correction, providing the Fellowship with boats and practical counsel for navigating the Anduin. In one of the book's warmer moments, Celeborn "gazed at the Dwarf in wonder" when Gimli, asked what gift he desires, requests three strands of Galadriel's hair.

---

## 32. Galadriel — terrible and beautiful beyond enduring

Galadriel is the culminating encounter of Frodo's journey, and Tolkien gives her one of his most layered descriptions. She is **very tall**—no less than Celeborn—**clad wholly in white**, with **hair of deep gold** that, according to *The Silmarillion*, captures the radiance of the Two Trees of Valinor, mingling gold and silver light. There is no sign of age upon her "unless it were in the depths of her eyes," which Tolkien describes as **"keen as lances in the starlight, and yet profound, the wells of deep memory."** She is called "the mightiest and fairest of all the Elves that remained in Middle-earth" and "the greatest of elven women."

Her personality is penetrating and formidable. When she meets the Fellowship, she gives each member a long, searching look, seeming to test their hearts, offering each a silent choice between their deepest desire and the difficult road ahead. Boromir is particularly shaken. She communicates **telepathically** with Frodo, reads thoughts, and speaks with formal, elevated diction that can shift from quiet counsel to apocalyptic grandeur in a breath.

The scene at **the Mirror of Galadriel** is the book's most psychologically charged encounter. She leads Frodo and Sam to a silver basin in a garden glade, fills it from a stream with a silver pitcher, and the Mirror shows visions of past, present, and possible future—including the **Eye of Sauron**, "rimmed with fire… yellow as a cat's… watchful and intent, and the black slit of its pupil opened on a pit, a window into nothing." When Frodo offers her the Ring, she rises to her full stature: **"She lifted up her hand and from the ring that she wore there issued a great light that illuminated her alone and left all else dark. She stood before Frodo seeming now tall beyond measurement, and beautiful beyond enduring, terrible and worshipful."** She envisions herself as a Queen "beautiful and terrible as the Morning and the Night," then laughs, and the light fades: she becomes once more **"a slender elf-woman, clad in simple white, whose gentle voice was soft and sad."** Her refusal is among Tolkien's finest lines: **"I will diminish, and go into the West, and remain Galadriel."**

Her parting gift to Frodo—the Phial of Galadriel, a small crystal vial containing the light of Eärendil's star—becomes his most precious possession: "a light for you in dark places, when all other lights go out."

## Creatures and powers witnessed but not truly "met"

Three additional beings deserve mention, though none are named characters in the conventional sense. At **Weathertop**, when Frodo puts on the Ring, he sees the Nazgûl in their true forms: five tall figures with "white faces" in which "burned keen and merciless eyes," wearing grey robes and silver helms, with swords of steel in "haggard hands." The Witch-king is distinguishable—"taller than the others: his hair was long and gleaming and on his helm was a crown." At the **West Gate of Moria**, the Watcher in the Water seizes Frodo with "a long sinuous tentacle… pale-green and luminous and wet," its full form never revealed. And on the **Bridge of Khazad-dûm**, Frodo witnesses the Balrog: "a great shadow, in the middle of which was a dark form, of man-shape maybe, yet greater… Its streaming mane kindled, and blazed behind it. In its right hand was a blade like a stabbing tongue of fire; in its left it held a whip of many thongs." Its shadow "reached out like two vast wings" from wall to wall—a being of the same angelic order as Gandalf himself, but long since fallen into fire and darkness.

## What Tolkien's descriptions reveal about his art

Reading these descriptions in sequence reveals a consistent pattern in Tolkien's characterization. **Hobbits are defined by personality, speech, and action** rather than physical appearance—Sam's devotion, Merry's intelligence, Pippin's impulsiveness, and Fatty's quiet courage matter far more than what any of them look like. **Men are described with greater physical specificity**—Aragorn's weather-beaten face, Boromir's silver collar and great horn—because in Tolkien's world, Men are defined by what they do with their mortal bodies. **Elves are rendered through light, radiance, and atmosphere** rather than conventional features: Gildor's company carries moonlight at their feet, Glorfindel blazes like a white flame through a veil, and Galadriel shifts between cosmic terror and gentle sadness. **Dwarves are characterized through craft, pride, and emotional intensity** rather than detailed portraiture. And the strangest beings—Bombadil, Goldberry, the Barrow-wight—are described through nature imagery, sound, and sensation rather than anatomy.

This is not accidental. Tolkien understood that the most memorable characters in literature are not those whose every feature is catalogued, but those whose presence is felt—whose manner of speech, movement, and being creates an atmosphere the reader inhabits. From the Gaffer's fireside gossip to Galadriel's starlit mirror, from Strider's gleaming eyes in a dark corner to Tom Bombadil's yellow boots stomping through the reeds, Tolkien builds character not as portrait but as experience. The reader does not merely see these figures; the reader, like Frodo, encounters them.
